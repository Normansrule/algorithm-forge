How to read this

Points are labelled p1, p2, … from left to right. The thick green segment is the closest pair found so far; thick green edges are hull edges already confirmed. A dashed yellow segment is the pair being measured right now.

Closest pair, divide-and-conquer. The light blue band is the current subproblem, the orange dashed line is the dividing line x = m, and the purple band is the strip of width 2d around it. For a point in the strip, the blue box is the region (d high) where a closer partner could still be; points above it are never checked.

Convex hull. The grey dashed line is the line being tested. In brute force, blue points are on its left and yellow points on its right: points on both sides mean "not an edge". In Quickhull, the red triangle is a pmax triangle: points inside it are eliminated (grey) for good.

Basic operations. Closest pair counts squared-distance computations (Levitin avoids square roots inside the loops). Convex hull counts orientation tests: the sign of the determinant x1y2 + x3y1 + x2y3 − x3y2 − x2y1 − x1y3, which says which side of a line a point is on.

Try this

  1. Quadratic versus n log n. With n = 40 random points, compare the squared-distance counts in the table: brute force is always n(n−1)/2 = 780; divide-and-conquer usually needs fewer than 60.
  2. The strip is thin. Step through divide-and-conquer and watch the strip checks: each strip point is compared with only a few neighbours above it, never more than a handful. Why can't there be more? (Levitin Exercise 5.5.2.)
  3. Quickhull's bad day. Press "On a circle": every point is on the hull, nothing is ever eliminated, and the orientation tests climb. Then press "Clusters": many points die inside the first triangles.
  4. Brute-force hull by hand. Clear the stage and click 5 points: 4 corners of a square and 1 in the middle. Predict how many of the 10 segments are hull edges (4), then step through.
  5. Early exit. In brute-force hull, a segment through the middle of the cloud is rejected after only a few tests, while a real hull edge needs all n − 2. How close is the count to the n(n−1)(n−2)/2 bound?
